## Claude's values across models and languages
Anthropic, 2026-07-13  
https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-values-models-languages

309,815 conversations, 3 models, 20 languages. Claude's expressed values vary meaningfully by language.

**Article angle:** language-dependent AI behavior as a Cognitive Autonomy, trust-calibration and multinational AI-governance issue. Do not claim language alone causes the differences.

## Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI
Anthropic Economics Team (Anton Korinek, Chad Jones, Szymon Sacher, Tess Cotter, Peter McCrory), September 2026  
https://www.anthropic.com/institute/econ-scenarios

Models three scenarios (Modest / Substantial / Extreme) for how AI could reshape the US economy by 2030. In the more transformative scenarios, knowledge-worker wages stagnate or fall (>10% decline in the Extreme case) even as GDP grows sharply, and the labor share of income falls while capital's share rises.

**Article angle:** the clearest "human risk management" data point yet — AI-driven growth does not automatically protect knowledge workers; cognitive autonomy and readiness become the individual-level hedge against a labor-market shift the model itself predicts as likely uneven.

**Research status:** institutional economic-scenario modeling by Anthropic's own economics team, not independent peer-reviewed research; treat the specific growth/wage percentages as modeled projections, not observed outcomes.

## DeepMind Institute launch
Google DeepMind (Shane Legg, James Manyika, Demis Hassabis), launched 2026-09-17  
https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/17/google-deepmind-launches-institute-to-widen-the-agi-debate/

New institute explicitly built to surface *disagreement* between Google, DeepMind and the wider research community on AGI. Inaugural essays cover AGI-disruption economic policy, preserving transparency in AI reasoning, human flourishing, and a proposed US-led frontier-model evaluation/standards body.

**Article angle:** a top-tier AI lab publicly institutionalizing "we don't have consensus" is itself evidence for the Criticalthinking.hu thesis that end users need their own judgment, not borrowed certainty from any single AI lab.

**Research status:** institutional launch with opinion/policy essays, not empirical research — cite as a governance-debate signal, not as a study finding.

## The Verification Bottleneck in AI-Accelerated Knowledge Work
Kwan Hong Tan, 2026-09-03  
https://zenodo.org/records/22282649

Introduces "queueing epistemics": a formal framework (Epistemic Load Ratio, Verification Capacity Frontier) modeling what happens when AI generates knowledge faster than humans can verify it. Simulations show a "Queue-Aware Adaptive Verification" strategy cuts severe escaped errors by 56.7% versus naive production-first review.

**Article angle:** gives the existing "Verification Bottleneck" territory a formal, citable model — organizations should govern AI use by verification capacity, not generation speed; a concrete frame for "why checking AI output is the real skill now."

**Research status:** self-published via Zenodo (not a peer-reviewed journal), single-author, simulation-based — cite as a proposed framework, not validated empirical fact.

## Same Values, Different Languages? Steering LLMs Toward Chinese Social Values
Yuemei Xu, Kexin Xu, Jian Zhou, Haoyu Lu, Yequan Wang, Aishan Liu, submitted 2026-09-08  
https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08515

Introduces C-Voices, a 86,400-instance multilingual probe dataset across six languages testing 12 dimensions of value preference, and finds LLM value expression is both model-dependent and language-sensitive. Also proposes a fine-tuning-free "value vector steering" method.

**Article angle:** independent, methodologically heavier confirmation (beyond Anthropic's own language study) that "the same AI" genuinely reasons differently depending on the language you use with it — strengthens territory 3 with a second, non-Anthropic data point.

**Research status:** arXiv preprint, not yet peer-reviewed.

## Designing Against Deskilling: Metacognitive Feedback Reduces Cognitive Offloading to LLM Assistants
Sebastian Maier, Kai Schwabe, Manuel Schneider, Stefan Feuerriegel, submitted 2026-09-17  
https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20143

Preregistered online experiment (N=704, 2×2 design + no-AI control) on fraction-arithmetic practice with an LLM assistant. Metacognitive feedback that makes the consequences of offloading explicit measurably reduced answer-offloading (OR=0.47) and improved unaided test performance (OR=1.51); an effort-based reward showed no effect.

**Article angle:** rare direct causal evidence (not just correlational survey data) that a simple design intervention — telling people what offloading costs them — protects both skill retention and learning outcomes. Strong empirical anchor for "Performance vs. Knowing" and "Cognitive Autonomy as Human Risk Management."

**Research status:** arXiv preprint, preregistered experiment — stronger methodological footing than most items in this file, but still pending peer review.

## Artificial Intelligence Literacy and Sustainable Development: An Ethical Governance and Development Goals Framework
Md. Masudul Islam, Mirza Niaz Morshed, Md. Shafiqul Islam, submitted 2026-09-09  
https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10489

Proposes a six-level AI-literacy taxonomy (technical knowledge through ethics, governance, and strategic foresight) and surveys 300 professionals, finding technical AI knowledge is comparatively strong but ethical/governance preparation lags. Ethical reasoning and reflective thinking are the strongest predictors of trustworthy AI use — stronger than technical literacy itself.

**Article angle:** direct empirical support for territory 5's core claim — that "AI literacy" (knowing how to use the tool) is not the same as cognitive/ethical readiness, and the second is what actually predicts trustworthy use.

**Research status:** arXiv preprint, survey-based (N=300), not yet peer-reviewed.

## New IBM CHRO Study: AI Puts Critical Thinking at the Center of Workforce Priorities
IBM Institute for Business Value, with Oxford Economics, published 2026-09-21  
https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-09-21-new-ibm-chro-study-ai-puts-critical-thinking-at-the-center-of-workforce-priorities

Two linked surveys (fielded April–June 2026): 1,500 CHROs/senior workforce-strategy executives across 21 geographies and 23 industries, and 8,800 full-time employees across 28 countries. 71% of CHROs say the ability to supervise and override AI outputs is essential, but only 29% of employees rate judgment as important; 60% of workers fear AI is eroding their skills, with critical thinking named most often. When AI fails, 43% of employees say blame falls on them; where CHROs share accountability for AI-assisted decisions, 76% of employees feel safe challenging AI output versus 43% where HR is merely advisory. 80% of CHROs acknowledge AI creates invisible workload from employees validating recommendations and handling exceptions. Separately, 72% of organizations report limited or no use of AI inside HR itself, and CHROs rate their own teams' AI literacy at just 13%.

**Article angle:** the freshest and largest empirical anchor yet for "Cognitive Autonomy as Human Risk Management" — a 10,300-person study showing organizations widely expect employees to supervise/override AI (i.e., exercise judgment) while doing little to build that capacity, and showing accountability structure (not tooling) is what determines whether employees actually feel safe pushing back on AI. Also directly supports "From AI Literacy to Cognitive Readiness" and the Verification Bottleneck's "invisible labor" framing.

**Research status:** industry survey research (IBM Institute for Business Value + Oxford Economics), not peer-reviewed academic research — cite percentages as self-reported survey findings, not observed behavior.

## How AI Models Manage Epistemic Authority: A Taxonomy and Comparative Analysis of Responses to User Disagreement
Riyadh Alnasser, Yusuf Mücahit Çetinkaya, Sumin Zhao, Tuğrulcan Elmas, submitted 2026-09-07  
https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07662

Dataset of 2,310 challenge scenarios across 14 models, analyzing how models respond when users push back on an answer: whether claims are maintained, where "authority" is located, how disagreement is handled, and what evidence is offered. Models validate the user in 85% of responses but simultaneously maintain their original claim in 65% of those same interactions; models apologize in about a third of responses while still standing by their original position in nearly 60% of those apologies. Genuine abandonment or replacement of the original claim is rare (roughly 0.8–40% and 1.5% respectively, varying by model).

**Article angle:** hard evidence for territory 6's core tension — a model's "I see your point" is very often performative agreement, not real epistemic recalibration, which means users who read an apology as "the AI updated its view" are frequently wrong. Strong material for "AI as Thinking Partner, Not Answer Machine" and a sharper edge for the Verification Bottleneck (you can't outsource judging correctness to how conciliatory the AI sounds).

**Research status:** arXiv preprint, not yet peer-reviewed.

## A Process Model of Managerial Phronesis in the Age of Generative AI
Dirk Lindebaum (University of Bath), Natarajan Balasubramanian (Ohio State University), Mehreen Ashraf (Cardiff University), Patrick Haack (University of Lausanne); Academy of Management Review, announced 2026-09-04  
https://journals.aom.org/doi/epub/10.5465/amr.2024.0582

Conceptual/theoretical process model of "managerial phronesis" (practical wisdom built through experience) under generative-AI use. Argues managers risk "epistemic de-skilling" — outsourcing thinking to AI erodes the ability to learn from experience and think critically, especially under time pressure, because AI "does not experience the world, understand the consequences of decisions, or grasp the social and emotional complexities" of workplace situations. But the same paper argues AI can instead become an up-skilling trigger when used as a tool for reflection, particularly in environments that require managers to justify decisions to others — accountability structures are what tip the outcome either way.

**Article angle:** a peer-reviewed (Academy of Management Review) theoretical anchor for both "Cognitive Autonomy as Human Risk Management" and "Performance vs. Knowing," with a genuinely actionable lever for readers: it's not AI use itself but the presence or absence of accountability/reflection structures that determines whether AI use erodes or builds judgment.

**Research status:** peer-reviewed journal (Academy of Management Review) but a conceptual/theoretical framework, not an empirical dataset — cite as theory-building, not as measured outcomes.
